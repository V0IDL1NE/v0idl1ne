## The project

In the mid-1700s, a Paris writer named **Denis Diderot** took over a project that was supposed to be a simple translation of an English encyclopedia. He turned it into something much bigger: the *Encyclopédie*, an attempt to collect everything people knew in one place, written so that anyone who could read could use it.

What made it different wasn't just the philosophy and science. It was the **trades**. The *Encyclopédie* was the first general encyclopedia to describe the "mechanical arts" — the skills of weavers, printers, glassmakers, metalworkers — in real detail. Diderot spent his days in workshops interviewing craftsmen and watching how things were actually made, then wrote it up at night. The finished work included thousands of engraved plates showing tools and processes step by step.

Knowledge that had been passed down inside guilds and families was now on a page, for anyone.

## Diderot already had a record

Before the first volume came out, Diderot had already been locked up. In 1749 he was arrested over a book called *Letter on the Blind* and held in solitary confinement in the fortress of Vincennes for months. He came out knowing exactly how dangerous the project was.

The first volume of the *Encyclopédie* was published in **1751**.

## The ban

By early **1752**, after the second volume, a royal decree suspended the work, accusing it of "destroying royal authority" and "fomenting a spirit of independence and revolt." An order went out to seize the manuscripts for the volumes still to come.

Here's the twist. The official in charge of the book trade — the man responsible for censorship, **Chrétien-Guillaume de Lamoignon de Malesherbes** — was quietly on Diderot's side. According to an account by Diderot's daughter, Malesherbes warned Diderot about the seizure ahead of time and took the papers into his own family's home, where nobody would think to look for them: the house of the censor himself.

## Banned again, and finished anyway

The project restarted, but in **1759**, after the seventh volume, the government revoked its permission to publish entirely. The Pope placed it on the Index of Forbidden Books.

Diderot kept going in secret, supported by highly placed people who wanted it finished. In 1764 he discovered that his own publisher, terrified of a crackdown, had been secretly cutting passages out of his articles before printing.

The last text volumes came out in 1765, and the final volumes of illustrations in **1772** — about twenty years after the first volume. The finished *Encyclopédie* ran to **28 volumes, roughly 72,000 articles, and nearly 2,800 plates.** Diderot described its purpose as changing the way people think.

## Why this is the first story here

The *Encyclopédie* wasn't dangerous because it was radical. It was dangerous because it was **useful**. It took knowledge that gave a small group of people an advantage and handed it to everybody.

That's still how a lot of practical knowledge works. Your rights when police stop you, how interest is calculated, what a landlord can't do, how to read your own pay stub — none of it is secret, exactly. It's just scattered, buried in statutes and fine print, and almost never taught. The people on the other side of the table know it. You're expected not to.
