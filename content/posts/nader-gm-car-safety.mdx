Seat belts, recalls you can look up by VIN, federal crash standards — all of it feels like it's always been there. It hasn't. It took a young lawyer, a book, and a car company that overplayed its hand.

---

## The book

In **November 1965**, a 31-year-old lawyer named **Ralph Nader** published *Unsafe at Any Speed.* Its argument was simple and explosive: American carmakers knew how to make cars safer and were choosing style and cost over lives.

Its most famous chapter went after the **Chevrolet Corvair**, a General Motors car, and its handling.

## The surveillance

General Motors didn't just argue with the book. It hired private investigators to look into **Nader himself.**

According to Nader's later lawsuit, investigators interviewed his friends and acquaintances, asking about his political, social, racial, and religious views; had him followed; and **tapped his phone.** He also alleged that women were sent to approach him in an attempt to set him up.

## It backfired

The surveillance came out. On **March 22, 1966**, GM's president, **James Roche**, appeared before a U.S. Senate subcommittee and **publicly apologized** to Nader.

The scandal made Nader famous and made his book a bestseller. That same year, Congress passed the **National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act**, which for the first time let the federal government set mandatory safety standards for cars. The agency now known as the **National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA)** grew out of it.

Nader sued GM for invasion of privacy, and in 1970 settled for **$425,000** — which he used to fund consumer advocacy work, including keeping watch on the auto industry.

## What this means for you

The system that came out of this fight is free and most people never use it.

- **Look up recalls on your car** at nhtsa.gov/recalls using your VIN (it's on your registration and at the bottom of the windshield). Recall repairs are done **free** by the dealer.
- **Report a safety problem** at nhtsa.gov too. Complaints from regular drivers are how investigations start.
- **If your new car keeps going back for the same defect**, your state's lemon law may require a buyback. [How lemon laws work.](/blog/lemon-law-basics)
