Even a minor crash scrambles your thinking. The problem is that what you do in the first hour — especially what you document and what you say — shapes the insurance claim that follows for months. Here's the order.

---

## 1. Stop, and make it safe

- **Stop.** Leaving the scene of an accident you were involved in — even one you didn't cause, even with no injuries — is a crime in every state
- **Check for injuries and call 911** if anyone is hurt, if a car can't be moved, or if the other driver seems impaired or hostile
- **Hazard lights on.** If the cars can be driven and nobody's hurt, many states want you to move out of traffic to the shoulder or a nearby lot — standing in live lanes is how second accidents happen

## 2. Exchange information

Every state requires drivers involved in a crash to exchange basic information. Get:

- Name, phone number, and address
- Driver's license number
- Insurance company and policy number (photograph their card)
- License plate, make, model, and color of every vehicle involved
- If the driver isn't the owner, the owner's name too

Also get names and phone numbers of any **witnesses** — they tend to leave within minutes, and an independent witness can settle a fault dispute.

## 3. Document everything

Use your phone before anything gets moved, if it's safe:

- **Wide shots** showing where the cars ended up relative to the road, signs, and signals
- **Close-ups** of every damaged area on every vehicle
- **The other car's plate**
- Skid marks, debris, road conditions, weather, and anything blocking visibility
- Any visible injuries

Then, while it's fresh, write or voice-record what happened: time, direction you were traveling, speed, what you saw.

## 4. Talk carefully

Be polite, and be factual. Don't say "I'm sorry" or "it was my fault" — even as a reflex. Fault is a legal and insurance determination made later with all the evidence, and your words at the scene can be used in it. Stick to exchanging information and describing facts.

## 5. Police report

Whether you *must* call police for a minor crash depends on your state — most require a report when there's an injury, a death, or damage above a set dollar amount (often somewhere between $500 and $2,500). Even when it's not required, a police report is useful neutral documentation, especially if the other driver's story might change. If officers don't come to the scene, ask how to file a report yourself, and get the report number.

## 6. Afterward

- **Get checked out** if anything hurts, even mildly — some injuries (like whiplash) show up a day or two later, and a prompt medical record matters for any claim
- **Notify your insurer promptly.** Most policies require it, even if you think the other driver is at fault
- **Don't give a recorded statement to the other driver's insurance company** until you've talked to your own insurer — you're generally not obligated to
- **Keep a folder:** photos, the report number, claim numbers, repair estimates, medical bills, and a log of every call

---

## Quick reference

1. **Stop — leaving the scene is a crime everywhere**
2. **Call 911 for injuries; move out of traffic if you safely can**
3. **Exchange license, insurance, plate, and contact info; get witnesses**
4. **Photograph everything before it's moved**
5. **Be polite, stick to facts — don't apologize or admit fault**
6. **Report to police if required in your state (and often even if not)**
7. **Notify your insurer; get checked out if anything hurts**
