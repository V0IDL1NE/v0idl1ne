Scams work on smart people because they're built to rush you. If you've just realized you were scammed, the same thing is true in reverse: the faster you act, the more you can undo. Embarrassment is normal — skip it for now. Here's the order.

---

## 1. Stop the money, based on how you paid

Call the company that moved the money **right now**, using the number on the back of your card or on its official website — not a number the scammer gave you.

| How you paid | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Credit card** | Call the issuer, report fraud, and dispute the charge. You have strong federal protections here |
| **Debit card or bank transfer** | Call your bank's fraud line immediately. If a scammer used your card or account without you, federal law limits your loss when you report fast (as little as $50 within 2 business days). If you were tricked into sending the money yourself, protections are weaker — but banks sometimes still help, so report it anyway |
| **Zelle, Venmo, Cash App, or another payment app** | Report it in the app *and* call your bank. Recovery isn't guaranteed, but it's more likely the faster you report |
| **Wire transfer** | Call the sending bank immediately and ask it to recall the wire. Hours matter |
| **Gift cards** | Call the company that issued the card (the number on the back), say it was used in a scam, and ask for a refund. Keep the card and receipt |
| **Cryptocurrency** | Contact the exchange you used right away. Crypto is very hard to recover, but report it anyway |
| **Check** | Call your bank to stop payment if it hasn't cleared |

## 2. Lock down anything they could reach

- **Did you give them a password?** Change it immediately — and on any other account that used the same one. Turn on two-factor authentication
- **Did you let them onto your computer** (a "tech support" call)? Disconnect it from the internet, uninstall any remote-access program they had you install, and run a security scan. Change passwords from a different, clean device
- **Did you give them your Social Security number or ID?** Go to **IdentityTheft.gov** for a personal recovery plan, and **freeze your credit** at all three bureaus — it's free
- **Did you give them a one-time code?** Assume they got into that account. Change its password and check its recent activity and settings

## 3. Report it

- **ReportFraud.ftc.gov** — the FTC's central scam database, used by law enforcement nationwide
- **IC3.gov** — the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center, for scams that happened online
- **Your state attorney general's** consumer protection office
- The platform where it happened — the marketplace, dating site, or social network

Reports rarely get your money back directly, but they build the cases that shut scam operations down — and a report number helps with bank disputes.

## 4. Watch for the second scam

Scam victims get targeted again, by "recovery" services promising to get your money back for an upfront fee — sometimes claiming to be from the FTC, the FBI, or a law firm. **Government agencies never charge to recover money**, and no legitimate recovery service can guarantee results. Anyone asking for a fee to get your money back is the next scam.

## 5. Save the evidence

Screenshots of messages, the phone numbers and email addresses they used, transaction receipts, and dates. Your bank and any police report will ask for it.

---

## Quick reference

1. **Call your bank or card company now** — use the number on your card, not one from the scammer
2. **Change any password you shared, and turn on 2FA**
3. **Gave your SSN? IdentityTheft.gov + freeze your credit**
4. **Report to ReportFraud.ftc.gov and IC3.gov**
5. **Anyone charging a fee to "recover" your money is scamming you again**
